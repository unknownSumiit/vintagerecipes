How to Read Old Recipe Cards: A Guide to Vintage Cooking Terms

By the Vintage Recipe Box team · Updated November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Few things are as precious as a recipe card written in a mother’s or grandmother’s handwriting. But anyone who has tried to cook from one knows that old recipes can be puzzling. They often call for “a moderate oven,” “a scant cup,” or “butter the size of an egg,” and some leave out the baking time entirely, because the cook simply knew.

This guide will help you translate those treasured old recipes into measurements and temperatures that work in a modern kitchen.

Oven temperatures

Many older recipes were written for wood or coal stoves, or for early ovens without accurate thermostats, so they describe heat in words rather than degrees. These are the generally accepted modern equivalents:

Old recipe saysUse this temperature
Very slow oven250°F to 275°F
Slow oven300°F to 325°F
Moderate oven350°F to 375°F
Quick oven or hot oven400°F to 425°F
Very hot oven450°F to 500°F

When a recipe gives no temperature at all, 350°F is a sensible starting point for most cakes, quick breads, and casseroles. Check early and often the first time you bake it.

Old measurementApproximate modern amount
Butter the size of an eggAbout 1/4 cup (4 tablespoons)
Butter the size of a walnutAbout 2 tablespoons
A teacupAbout 3/4 cup
A coffee cupAbout 1 cup
A gill1/2 cup
A wineglassAbout 1/4 cup
A saltspoonAbout 1/4 teaspoon
A scant cupA little less than a full cup, about 2 tablespoons short
A heaping cup or spoonfulFilled above the rim rather than leveled off

Can sizes

Mid-century recipes often call for cans by number. These are the standard sizes:

  • No. 303 can: about 16 ounces, or 2 cups
  • No. 2 can: about 20 ounces, or 2 1/2 cups
  • No. 2 1/2 can: about 29 ounces, or 3 1/2 cups
  • No. 10 can: about 6 1/2 pounds, the large size used by restaurants and church kitchens

Modern cans are often slightly smaller than their older equivalents, so when a recipe calls for “1 can,” the closest common size today will almost always work.

Ingredients that have changed names

  • Oleo: margarine.
  • Sweet milk: ordinary fresh milk, as opposed to sour milk or buttermilk.
  • Sour milk: milk that has soured naturally. Today, use buttermilk, or stir 1 tablespoon of lemon juice or vinegar into a cup of milk and let it stand for 5 minutes.
  • Sweet butter: unsalted butter.
  • Lard or shortening: still available. Many bakers swear by them for flaky pie crusts.
  • Saleratus or soda: baking soda.
  • Graham flour: a coarse whole-wheat flour. Regular whole-wheat flour is the closest substitute.
  • Confectioners’ sugar or XXXX sugar: powdered sugar.
  • Pie plant: rhubarb.

Cooking terms you may not recognize

  • Dredge: to coat lightly with flour.
  • Scald (milk): to heat until tiny bubbles form around the edge, just below boiling. Older recipes did this to improve texture in breads and custards; with today’s pasteurized milk, simply warming it is usually enough.
  • Cream (butter and sugar): to beat together until light and fluffy.
  • Cut in: to work cold fat into flour with a pastry blender or two knives until it looks like coarse crumbs.
  • Bake until done: the cook was expected to know. For cakes and breads, a toothpick inserted in the center should come out clean. For meats, use a thermometer.

A few tips for cooking from old cards

  1. Read the whole card first and jot modern measurements in pencil next to the old ones.
  2. Make the recipe once exactly as written, then adjust. Flour, ovens, and pan sizes have changed, so small tweaks are normal.
  3. Use modern food safety guidelines for temperatures and storage, even when the old card does not mention them.
  4. Ask older relatives while you can. They may remember details that never made it onto the card.
