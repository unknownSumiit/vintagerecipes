Recipes by Era: A Taste of American Kitchen History

From 1800s baked macaroni to 1960s cocktail-party meatballs, here is the story behind each recipe in our collection, roughly in the order these dishes found their place on American tables.

1800s classic
1800s classic · Supper Classics

Old-Fashioned Baked Macaroni and Cheese

Baked macaroni and cheese has been on American tables far longer than the blue box. Thomas Jefferson served macaroni dishes at the White House, and a recipe for baked macaroni with cheese appeared in Mary Randolph’s The Virginia House-Wife in 1824. By the mid-1900s, a golden casserole of baked macaroni was a Sunday dinner and holiday staple.

1800s classic · Grandma’s Pies

Lemon Meringue Pie

The American lemon meringue pie is often credited to Elizabeth Goodfellow, who ran a cooking school in Philadelphia in the early 1800s. By the 20th century it was a diner counter and church supper favorite, admired for its sunny, tart filling and its tall, toasted crown of meringue.

1850s
1850s · Old-Fashioned Cakes

Old-Fashioned Strawberry Shortcake

Strawberry shortcake has been an American summer tradition since the mid-1800s, when towns held “strawberry shortcake socials” to celebrate the berry harvest. The original shortcake is not a sponge cake but a tender, lightly sweet biscuit, split and piled with juicy berries and cream.

Timeless · Grandma’s Pies

Classic Double-Crust Apple Pie

Apple pie came to America with English and Dutch settlers, and apple orchards soon spread across the country. By the early 1900s the phrase “as American as apple pie” was in common use, and during World War II, soldiers were said to tell reporters they were fighting “for mom and apple pie.”

Farmhouse classic
Farmhouse classic · Grandma’s Pies

Strawberry Rhubarb Pie

Rhubarb was so commonly baked into pies on American farms that it earned the nickname “pie plant.” Because it is one of the first crops of spring, a rhubarb pie was a welcome treat after a long winter, and pairing it with the season’s first strawberries became a farmhouse tradition.

Immigrant classic
Immigrant classic · Supper Classics

Grandma’s Stuffed Cabbage Rolls

Stuffed cabbage came to America with immigrants from Poland, Ukraine, Hungary, and across Eastern Europe, where it goes by names like gołąbki and holubtsi. In the steel and mill towns of Pennsylvania, Ohio, and the Midwest, these rolls became a beloved Sunday dinner and a fixture at church fundraisers and weddings.

Bake sale classic · Potluck & Church Supper Favorites

Homemade Cinnamon Rolls

Cinnamon rolls trace their roots to the Swedish kanelbulle and similar Northern European sweet breads. In America, they became a bake sale, church breakfast, and holiday morning tradition, baked from scratch in a 9x13 pan and passed down on well-worn recipe cards.

1930s–40s
1930s–40s · Old-Fashioned Cakes

Depression-Era Wacky Cake

Wacky cake, also called crazy cake or Depression cake, is a moist chocolate cake made without eggs, butter, or milk. It became popular during the hard years of the 1930s and the rationing of World War II, when those ingredients were scarce or expensive. Vinegar and baking soda make it rise, and it is famously mixed right in the baking pan.

Potluck classic
Potluck classic · Potluck & Church Supper Favorites

Classic Deviled Eggs

In the 1700s, “deviled” came to describe foods made zesty with spices or mustard. Stuffed eggs had been around for centuries, but deviled eggs became a true American institution in the mid-1900s, when no potluck, picnic, or holiday buffet was complete without a special egg plate lined with them.

1950s picnic
1950s picnic · Potluck & Church Supper Favorites

Old-Fashioned Macaroni Salad

Creamy macaroni salad rose to fame alongside the American backyard cookout in the years after World War II, when jarred mayonnaise and inexpensive dried pasta were in every pantry. It became the faithful companion to hot dogs, fried chicken, and barbecue at picnics and family reunions across the country.

1950s · Supper Classics

Salisbury Steak with Mushroom Gravy

Salisbury steak is named for Dr. James Salisbury, a 19th-century American physician who recommended ground beef patties to his patients. The dish became a household name in the 1950s and 1960s as a diner and TV-dinner favorite, but it has always been at its best homemade, smothered in onion and mushroom gravy.

1950s holiday · Grandma’s Pies

Old-Fashioned Pumpkin Pie

Pumpkin pie has graced American Thanksgiving tables since colonial times, but the version most families know today took shape in the 1950s, when a smooth custard made with canned pumpkin and evaporated milk became the standard printed on can labels and in women’s magazines across the country.

1950s–60s
1950s–60s · Potluck & Church Supper Favorites

Sour Cream Coffee Cake

The fluted Bundt pan was introduced in Minnesota in 1950, and by the 1960s a tall, tender coffee cake swirled with cinnamon and nuts was the pride of church coffee hours, bridge clubs, and neighborly visits. Sour cream was the secret to keeping these cakes moist for days.

1950s–60s · Supper Classics

Old-Fashioned Swedish Meatballs

Swedish immigrants brought köttbullar to the Upper Midwest in the late 1800s, but Swedish meatballs truly became an American favorite in the 1950s and 1960s, when they were the star of cocktail parties, kept warm in a chafing dish and served with toothpicks. As a main course over egg noodles, they were a fancy-feeling dinner that was easy on the budget.
